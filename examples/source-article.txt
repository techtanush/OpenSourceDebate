Downtown Car Bans Cut Congestion Without Hurting Retail, Study Finds

By Dana Whitfield, Staff Writer, The Example Urban Review. Published March 3, 2025.

When the city of Larkspur closed its downtown core to private cars in 2019, local merchants held a protest outside city hall. Fifty storefront owners signed a letter warning that customers would simply drive to the suburban shopping center instead, and that half of them would be out of business within two years. Larkspur is one of twelve mid-sized cities now covered by a four-year analysis from the Example Transit Institute, and the results are not what the merchants predicted.

The study, led by transportation economist Dr. Marcus Reyes, followed each city for four years after its ban took effect and compared downtown sales-tax receipts, foot-traffic counts, and traffic volumes on the streets surrounding each ban zone against pre-ban baselines. The institute is a nonprofit research organization funded by city transportation agencies and two private foundations, and the full dataset has been posted publicly so other researchers can check the numbers.

"Merchants predicted disaster," Reyes said. "What we measured was the opposite. Foot traffic rose, and total sales were flat to slightly up in eleven of the twelve cities." Average travel times on the streets bordering each ban zone fell by nine percent, because drivers switched to transit or took different routes before ever reaching downtown. Reyes said the pattern surprised his own team, which had expected at least a modest rise in congestion on the boundary streets during the first year.

The report cautions that results depend heavily on transit quality. In the one city where sales fell, buses ran only every forty minutes and the ban took effect with no change to service. "A ban without a working alternative just moves the problem," Reyes said. Cities that paired bans with frequent service, meaning a bus or train at least every ten minutes during the day, saw the largest gains in both ridership and retail activity, and in those cities boundary-street traffic fell by as much as fourteen percent.

Critics say twelve cities is a small sample and that mid-sized cities may not be a guide for large metropolitan areas. Reyes agreed the results should not be stretched too far. "We would not tell a city of ten million people that this is a template," he said. "But for cities of a few hundred thousand, which is most cities, the fear that a ban will wreck downtown is not supported by what happened."

The institute recommends that cities phase in restrictions over two years and publish yearly data so residents can judge results for themselves. It also recommends keeping loading zones and accessible drop-off points inside the ban zone, noting that every city in the study that did so reported far fewer complaints from disabled residents and delivery businesses than the cities that did not.
