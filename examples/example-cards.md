# Example Aff Block File

## Congestion and retail

### A2: Bans just move traffic to surrounding streets

> Neg Argument: A downtown ban pushes drivers onto the surrounding streets, so congestion gets worse overall and nothing is solved.

> 1. Empirically, the streets around ban zones got faster, not slower, and where they did not it was because no transit alternative existed.

#### 2. Streets bordering ban zones got faster: travel times fell nine percent because drivers switched modes or routes before reaching downtown

The study, led by transportation economist Dr. Marcus Reyes, followed each city for four years after its ban took effect and compared downtown sales-tax receipts, foot-traffic counts, and traffic volumes on the streets surrounding each ban zone against pre-ban baselines. The institute is a nonprofit research organization funded by city transportation agencies and two private foundations, and the full dataset has been posted publicly so other researchers can check the numbers. "Merchants predicted disaster," Reyes said. "What we measured was the opposite. Foot traffic rose, and total sales were flat to slightly up in eleven of the twelve cities." ==Average travel times on the streets bordering each ban zone fell by nine percent, because drivers switched to transit or took different routes before ever reaching downtown.== Reyes said the pattern surprised his own team, which had expected at least a modest rise in congestion on the boundary streets during the first year. The report cautions that results depend heavily on transit quality. In the one city where sales fell, buses ran only every forty minutes and the ban took effect with no change to service. "A ban without a working alternative just moves the problem," Reyes said. Cities that paired bans with frequent service, meaning a bus or train at least every ten minutes during the day, saw the largest gains in both ridership and retail activity, and in those cities boundary-street traffic fell by as much as fourteen percent.

> Source note: a four-year study of twelve mid-sized cities by a nonprofit research institute funded by transportation agencies and foundations; the dataset is public. Weakness: small sample, mid-sized cities only.

### A2: Retail will collapse

> Neg Argument: Without cars, downtown customers go to the suburbs, so stores lose sales and close.

> 1. Empirically, the merchants' prediction was tested in twelve cities and did not happen: sales were flat or up in eleven.

When the city of Larkspur closed its downtown core to private cars in 2019, local merchants held a protest outside city hall. Fifty storefront owners signed a letter warning that customers would simply drive to the suburban shopping center instead, and that half of them would be out of business within two years. Larkspur is one of twelve mid-sized cities now covered by a four-year analysis from the Example Transit Institute, and the results are not what the merchants predicted. The study, led by transportation economist Dr. Marcus Reyes, followed each city for four years after its ban took effect and compared downtown sales-tax receipts, foot-traffic counts, and traffic volumes on the streets surrounding each ban zone against pre-ban baselines. The institute is a nonprofit research organization funded by city transportation agencies and two private foundations, and the full dataset has been posted publicly so other researchers can check the numbers. "Merchants predicted disaster," Reyes said. "What we measured was the opposite. ==Foot traffic rose, and total sales were flat to slightly up in eleven of the twelve cities.==" Average travel times on the streets bordering each ban zone fell by nine percent, because drivers switched to transit or took different routes before ever reaching downtown. Reyes said the pattern surprised his own team, which had expected at least a modest rise in congestion on the boundary streets during the first year.
