# Research Guide

How to research debate evidence well. The agent brings its own web search and page-reading tools; this guide says **what to look for, in what order, how to search for it, and how to decide what to keep**. Read `integrity.md` first (verbatim quotes, saved sources, UNVERIFIED labels) and `source-evaluation.md` (rating sources). `research-protocol.md` is the short checklist; this is the full method.

Worked examples below use the fictional topic "Resolved: Cities should ban private cars from downtown cores" so the queries are concrete. The method works on any topic.

---

## 1. Research is gap-driven, not topic-driven

Do not start by "searching the topic." Start with the **structure you need to fill**, then research each gap.

1. Write the chain map or the answer list first (`argument-structure.md`): for a contention, one slot per step (UQ, TREND, LINK, IL, IMPACT, SOLVENCY); for a block, one slot per answer you plan to make.
2. Turn each slot into a **research question** that a card could answer (table below).
3. Run searches for one slot at a time. Do not move on until the slot has a card, or you have decided to cut the slot.
4. Log what you did (section 9).

A topic search ("car-free downtowns") returns articles. A slot search ("injury rates after downtown car restrictions, before and after, matched comparison") returns the card.

---

## 2. What to look for, step by step

For each step: what the card must show, the strongest kinds of source, query templates, and red flags. Replace the bracketed words.

### UQ (uniqueness): how big is the problem right now?

- **Must show:** a current, specific number for the problem, ideally for the place or population your resolution affects.
- **Best sources:** official statistics and agency reports; annual reports; national or city data portals; investigative reporting that cites data.
- **Queries:**
  - `[problem] statistics [current year] report`
  - `[agency] annual report [problem] [place]`
  - `[problem] rate per 100,000 latest`
  - `[problem] [place] police data` / `hospital data` / `inspection data`
- **Red flags:** data older than three years on a fast-moving issue; a national number when the resolution is local (or the reverse); advocacy-group figures with no method.
- **Example:** `pedestrian injuries downtown share of city total annual report`

### TREND: is it getting worse, and how fast?

- **Must show:** direction and speed with dates: a time series or a forecast.
- **Best sources:** the same agencies' multi-year reports; planning documents with forecasts; research institutes' projections.
- **Queries:** `[problem] increase since [year]`, `[problem] projected [future year] forecast`, `[metric] year over year [place]`, `[problem] trend [first year]-[last year]`.
- **Red flags:** a forecast with no stated assumptions; a single-year spike presented as a trend.

### LINK: why does the resolution change the situation? (the most important step)

- **Must show:** a causal mechanism: the action causes the effect.
- **Best evidence, strongest first:**
  1. Controlled before-and-after studies or natural experiments (a comparison group, same period).
  2. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews of many cases.
  3. Evaluation reports of the same or very similar policy.
  4. Expert statements that explain the mechanism, from an expert in that field.
  5. Case studies and examples (use as a second card, not the only card).
- **Queries:**
  - `[action] effect on [outcome] before and after`
  - `[action] controlled study` / `difference-in-differences` / `natural experiment`
  - `[action] meta-analysis` / `systematic review` / `evidence review`
  - `evaluation of [policy] [outcome] results`
  - `why [action] reduces [outcome] mechanism`
- **Red flags:** correlation described as causation; a study of a different intervention (analogy) with no second source; "expert says" with no data; a link that only appears in a press release.
- **Example:** `downtown car restriction injury outcomes before after comparison streets bordering roads`

### IL (internal link): what does that change cause next?

- **Must show:** each next causal step, one card per step.
- **Best sources:** cost-of-harm studies, health-economics research, research linking the outcome to what you will claim next.
- **Queries:** `[outcome] causes [next effect] study`, `cost of [outcome] per case`, `[outcome] economic burden`, `effects of [outcome] on [population]`.
- **Red flags:** a leap to a dramatic effect with no card for the middle steps; a card about a different population.

### IMPACT: how large is the final harm or benefit?

- **Must show:** a number with a timeframe and who is affected.
- **Best sources:** national and global statistics bodies, major research institutes, official cost estimates.
- **Queries:** `annual [deaths/cases/cost] [problem] [country] latest`, `economic cost of [problem] per year`, `[problem] burden worldwide [organization]`, `[problem] highest in [N] years`.
- **Red flags:** a number with no year; "billions" with no source method; a worst-case projection presented as the expected case.

### SOLVENCY: does the resolution actually fix it?

- **Must show:** the resolution, as written, produces the reduction. This is about the resolution itself, not the problem (`argument-structure.md`).
- **Best evidence:** results from places that did what your resolution requires (with the same design features: phase-in, exemptions, funding); implementation evaluations; expert endorsements of the design; evidence on the specific provisions.
- **Queries:**
  - `[city/state/country] [policy] results [2 or 3] years after`
  - `[policy] implementation lessons learned evaluation`
  - `[policy] [outcome] reduced after implementation`
  - `what makes [policy] work` / `design features [policy] success`
- **Red flags:** solvency evidence from a policy that lacked a feature your plan depends on; results reported before the policy had time to work; "it should work" from someone with no track record.

### TURN and DA-link evidence

- **Turn:** find evidence that the opponent's own mechanism, applied to the resolution, produces the opposite of their claim. Search `[their claimed harm] decreases when [resolution action]`.
- **DA link (Neg):** the link card must be about what the resolution actually requires. Search `[resolution action] causes [trigger]`, then check the scope.

## 2b. The topic map: what to gather for any resolution

Before the slot-by-slot work, build a quick map of the **kinds of evidence a resolution needs**. For any topic, look for each of these and keep a one-line note and a source for each:

| Look for | Why you need it | Example question |
|----------|-----------------|------------------|
| **Definitions** of the key terms from an authority (statute, agency, dictionary of the field) | Frames the debate; prevents the opponent from redefining | "How does the law or agency define a hyperscale facility / a downtown core?" |
| **The status quo**: existing laws, programs, and trends | Uniqueness for both sides; shows what the resolution changes | "What already exists? What is already being done or planned?" |
| **Who is affected** and how many | Scale of both the benefit and the cost | "How many people, businesses, or places does this touch?" |
| **Harms now (quantitative)** | The Aff's problem; the Neg's baseline | "What does the problem cost in deaths, dollars, or damage per year?" |
| **Mechanisms**: how the action causes the effect | The link | "Why would this action change that outcome? What does the research say?" |
| **Precedents and empirics**: places that tried it | Solvency and counter-solvency | "Who has done this, and what happened afterward?" |
| **Costs and tradeoffs** | Neg disadvantages, Aff answers | "What does it cost, who pays, what does it displace?" |
| **Counterarguments in the literature** | Know the attack before they make it | "What do critics and opponents say, and what is their best evidence?" |
| **Expert and institutional positions** | Credible quotes and neutral backing | "What do the relevant agencies, associations, and researchers say?" |
| **Legal and practical feasibility** | Answers "that can't be done" | "Is it legal? What authority? What implementation problems are documented?" |
| **Timing and trends** | Urgency and the future impact | "Is it getting better or worse, how fast, and when does it matter?" |

**Kinds of evidence to collect** (mix them; one kind alone is fragile):

- **Statistics and official data:** best for UQ, trend, and impact.
- **Controlled or comparative studies:** best for links and solvency.
- **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses:** best for showing a pattern across many cases.
- **Evaluation reports of real policies:** best for solvency and counter-solvency.
- **Case studies and named examples:** a good second card; weak alone.
- **Expert opinion and statements:** best for explaining a mechanism or endorsing a design; weak without data.
- **Legal or regulatory text:** best for what the resolution requires and what already exists.
- **Projections and models:** useful for the future; state the assumptions.
- **Surveys and polling:** useful for public opinion and lay appeal.

Use the map to decide which contentions are strong (many kinds of evidence available) and which are thin, then go slot by slot.

## 3. How to search

1. **Three to five queries per slot**, worded differently: plain language, technical language, and a "results" form ("results", "evaluation", "study", "report").
2. **Search by source type.** Add `site:` filters for agency and research domains where your tool supports them (government domains, university domains, well-known institutes), or add the agency's name.
3. **Follow the citation upstream.** A news article about a study is a pointer. Find the study or report, read it, and cut from the primary source when possible.
4. **Read the limitations section.** Before keeping a card, find what the authors say it does not show. You will need it for the source note and for cross-ex.
5. **Check the date and any updates.** Use the most recent version of a report. Note "updated" dates.
6. **Find the same fact in a second independent source.** Two sources saying the same thing makes a strong pair of cards; one source is a risk.
7. **Save the full text** of the page you will cut from (`integrity.md`). If a page is blocked, follow the fallback there; never cut from a snippet.
8. **Write down the query that worked.** It is useful for the next slot.

### Reading a source quickly

- Skim headings, abstract or summary, key findings, methodology, limitations, and conclusions.
- Find the one passage that makes your slot's claim most directly. Read the paragraphs before and after it for context and qualifiers.
- Check that numbers in the headline match numbers in the body.

## 4. Collect candidates, then choose

For each slot, aim for **two or three candidate sources**, then choose the best and keep the runner-up as a backup card. Choose by:

| Criterion | Prefer |
|-----------|--------|
| Directness | Says it about your exact claim, not a nearby one |
| Credibility | Qualified author, neutral or relevant institution |
| Recency | Newest data that still applies |
| Scope | Same kind of place, population, and policy as your resolution |
| Method | Controlled, measured, with limits stated |
| Independence | Not funded by a party that benefits from the result (note it if it is) |

Keep a note for each chosen card: its main strength and its main weakness. The weakness becomes a one-line source note and a prepared answer.

---

## 5. Counter-research: know the attack before the opponent does

For every chain, run the other side's searches:

- Search for evidence **against** each link: `[action] fails`, `[action] no effect on [outcome]`, `[action] increased [outcome]`, `criticism of [study]`.
- Search for the **best objection** to your solvency evidence (a different place, a failed attempt).
- If you find strong counter-evidence, do one of: narrow your claim to what survives, add a card that answers it, or build the answer into your analytic. Do not ignore it. Opponents will find it.

---

## 6. Research for rebuttals and blocks: answer the card, not the topic

When you are answering an opposing case, the research target is **their specific cards**. For each card they read:

1. **Get the card text** (their file, a PDF, or the original source). Read the paragraphs before and after the highlighted part.
2. **Note author, date, scope, method, and funding.**
3. **Look for these holes, in this order:**

| Hole | Search or check | Example answer |
|------|-----------------|----------------|
| Their own text | Re-read the card for qualifiers, concessions, scope limits, the author's real conclusion | "Their card says 'in a pilot of 3 streets,' not citywide." |
| Scope mismatch | Compare the card's place/population/time to the resolution | "This study is about one neighborhood; the resolution covers a downtown." |
| Old data | Search for newer data on the same measure | "A 2025 report from the same agency shows the opposite trend." |
| Method | Read the methodology and limitations | "It has no comparison group." |
| Their author elsewhere | Search `[author] [topic]` for other statements | "The same author later wrote that X was overstated." |
| Counter-study | Search for studies with the opposite finding | "Eleven-city review: no effect." |
| Source bias | Check funding and mission | "Industry trade group, funded by the companies affected." |
| Internal inconsistency | Compare their card with their other cards | "Their grid card says supply is short; their economy card says demand is falling." |
| No link to the resolution | Compare the card's action to the actual resolution | "The card is about banning X; the resolution only pauses new construction." |

4. **Choose the strongest 2-4 answers**, strongest first, and find a card for each that does not depend on the others. See `refutation-guide.md` for how to turn these into a block.

Prefer answers to their **specific cards** over generic case takeouts, especially with lay judges (`judge-adaptation.md`).

## 7. Research from your own files first

Before searching the web, search what the user already has:

```bash
python tools/library.py search <folder> <terms...> --text
python tools/library.py index <folder> -o library-index.md
```

- **Reuse existing cards** that fit the slot. Do not re-cut the same article.
- **Merge, don't duplicate.** If a block for this argument already exists, add the new card to it (`file-organization.md`).
- **Know what was already read.** Cards in the case are "extendable" in frontlines.
- **Match the style** of the existing files (headings, cite format).

See `playbooks/06-use-my-files-as-context.md`.

## 8. When the evidence does not exist

Sometimes you cannot find a source that supports a claim. Do this:

1. **Say so plainly** to the user ("I could not find a source for X").
2. **Do not** change the number, the timeframe, or the quote to fit a weaker source, and do not present a snippet as the source.
3. **Offer a narrower claim** that the evidence does support ("reduces injuries 36% across 11 cities" instead of "saves lives").
4. **Mark it as a research gap** in the research log and the "Before you read these" list.

This is not a failure. It is correct, and it protects the user in cross-ex.

## 9. The research log

Keep `research-notes.md` (or the format the user prefers) as you work. For each slot:

```
Slot: LINK - restrictions reduce walking injuries
Queries run: "downtown car restriction injury before after", "car-free zone pedestrian injuries study",
             "low traffic neighbourhood injuries controlled analysis"
Candidates:  A) Venkataraman 25 (11-city review, 2025)   B) Fairhaven DPW 25 (single city, 2025)
Chosen:      A (broader, peer-reviewed). B kept as solvency card.
Rejected:    "Car-free Sunday" blog (anecdote, no data)
Strength:    controlled comparison across 11 cities
Weakness:    mid-sized cities only; observational
Saved:       sources/venkataraman-25.txt
```

At the end, summarize gaps and weak spots for the "Before you read these" list.

---

## 10. Effort and stopping

- **One card:** one good source and a quick check of its context.
- **A block:** three to five answers, each with a card, plus counter-research on your best answer.
- **A contention:** every step in the chain map has a card; the link and solvency are the hardest, so spend the most time there.
- **A case:** research the weakest contention's missing steps before polishing the strong one.
- Stop researching a slot when you have a strong card, a backup, and you know its weakness. More searching has diminishing returns; use the time to check the chain's flow (`logic-flow.md`).
