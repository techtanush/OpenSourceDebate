# Source Evaluation

Not all evidence is equal. Before cutting, rate the source.

## Checklist

1. **Qualifications.** Is the author an expert on *this* specific claim? A professor of economics on an economics claim is strong. A marketing executive on a technical claim is weak.
2. **Independence.** Does the author or organization profit from the conclusion? (A company selling GPU rental describing GPU waste; a trade group arguing for its industry; a staffing firm describing its own market.) Not disqualifying, but note it and pair with an independent source.
3. **Recency.** Newer is better for fast-changing facts (prices, capacity, legislation, polling). Very old evidence needs a reason it still applies.
4. **Type of source.**
   - Strongest: peer-reviewed research, government statistical agencies, official reports.
   - Strong: major newspapers' reporting, respected think tanks (note their lean), industry data providers.
   - Use with care: trade press republishing press releases, company blogs, opinion pieces, newsletters, social media.
   - Avoid: anonymous posts, content farms, pages with no author or date.
5. **Primary vs. secondary.** Prefer the original study, filing, or statement over an article about it. If you cite the article, check the original says the same.
6. **Direct vs. inferred.** Does the source say it, or do you have to stretch it? The more steps from the source to your tag, the weaker the card.
7. **Quotes of quotes.** A source reporting a number from another study is only as good as that study. Name both.

## How to handle weak sources

- Say so to the user in your "before you read these" notes.
- Look for a stronger source making the same point.
- If you keep it, make sure the card only claims what it actually says.
- Opponents attack bias, so prepare the answer ("same point from [independent source]").

## Predicting attacks

For each card, write one line: "If the opponent attacks this card, they'll say ___; we answer ___." If you can't answer, find a different card.
